Guide — Structural Health Monitoring

Vibration-based structural health monitoring, explained

A structure's own vibration carries information about its condition — if you know what to measure, and you don't trust any single measurement alone.

In short
Structural health monitoring uses sensors already on (or added to) a structure to continuously track its condition between manual inspections. Vibration-based methods specifically watch how a structure's natural frequencies shift over time — a stiffer structure vibrates at a higher frequency than a weakened one, a relationship published structural engineering research (Worden et al. 2012; Salawu 1997) has quantified. The reliable approach cross-checks that frequency-drift signal against others — strain, tilt/settlement, raw vibration — rather than trusting any one signal in isolation.

Why a structure's vibration tells you something

Every structure has natural frequencies it vibrates at under ambient excitation — wind, traffic, foot or vehicle load. Those frequencies are a direct function of the structure's mass and stiffness. Stiffness is what changes when a structure is damaged: a cracked beam, corroded reinforcement, or a loosened connection all reduce local stiffness before they're visible to the eye. Because frequency depends on stiffness, a real reduction in stiffness shows up as a small, measurable drop in the structure's natural frequency — often well before the damage is visually obvious during a routine inspection.

This relationship — using frequency drift as evidence of stiffness loss — is not a proprietary model. It's a well-published line of structural engineering research; Salawu's 1997 review and Worden et al.'s 2012 work on damage-identification methods are two of the standard references practitioners cite. That matters for anyone evaluating a monitoring system: the underlying math is something a structural engineer can look up and verify independently, not a black-box score you're asked to trust on faith.

Why one signal alone isn't enough

Frequency drift is powerful evidence, but it isn't the whole picture on its own. Ambient temperature changes a structure's stiffness slightly too, and a sensor can drift or fail in ways that look like a frequency shift but aren't structural at all. A monitoring approach that reads only one signal type has no independent way to tell a real structural issue apart from a temperature effect or a bad sensor reading. That's the core argument for cross-checking multiple, physically distinct signal types against each other: a real structural problem tends to show up in more than one of them at once, while a sensor artifact usually shows up in only one.

Four signals, cross-checked

Each of the following answers a different physical question about a structure's condition:

Vibration

Raw modal response — how the structure actually moves under load and ambient excitation. The base signal everything else is checked against.

Stiffness (frequency drift)

Shifts in modal frequency over time — the signal most directly tied to the published stiffness-loss methods (Worden 2012, Salawu 1997).

Strain

Direct load-path measurement at instrumented points — cross-checked against what the vibration signal implies about load distribution.

Tilt / settlement

Slow, cumulative geometric change — often the signal most likely to catch a foundation or support issue the other three wouldn't.

Why cross-checking matters, concretely
A sudden temperature swing can shift a structure's measured natural frequency by an amount that, read in isolation, looks similar to early-stage stiffness loss. But a temperature effect doesn't independently show up in strain or tilt/settlement data the same way real structural damage tends to. Cross-checking the four signals against each other is what lets a system tell the difference — reducing false alarms without simply raising the threshold and missing real early-stage damage.

What this produces in practice

The useful output isn't four separate charts a structural engineer has to reconcile by eye — it's one verdict: a stiffness-loss estimate, a damage-equivalent volume, and an inspection priority (for example, a "Watch" status meaning flagged for continued monitoring, not an immediate closure).
